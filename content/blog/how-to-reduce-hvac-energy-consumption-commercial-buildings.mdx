In most commercial buildings — offices, hotels, hospitals, campuses, manufacturing facilities — HVAC is the single largest line item on the electricity bill, typically 40-60% of total consumption. Most of that spend isn't necessary. It's the result of equipment running on fixed schedules and fixed setpoints, regardless of what the building actually needs at any given moment.

## Where the waste actually comes from

**Chillers running at fixed loading regardless of demand.** A chiller plant sized for peak summer load spends most of the year running well below that peak, but without active control it keeps operating in the same band of efficiency it was set up for, instead of the band that matches current demand.

**AHUs and fan coil units on static schedules.** Air handling units are frequently scheduled to run at fixed hours and fixed fan speeds, independent of actual occupancy or the difference between return air and setpoint. A meeting room empty on a Tuesday afternoon is still being conditioned at full capacity.

**No feedback loop between zones and plant.** Individual floors or zones might be over-cooled or under-cooled, and that information rarely makes its way back to the central plant in a way that changes how the plant runs.

**Manual setpoint changes that never get reverted.** A facility manager bumps a setpoint down two degrees during a hot week and it's never changed back. Multiply that across a year and a building, and it adds up to a meaningful chunk of avoidable consumption.

## What actually moves the needle

**1. Variable Frequency Drives (VFDs) on pumps and fans, actively controlled — not just installed.** A VFD alone doesn't save energy; it saves energy when something is continuously adjusting its speed to match real-time load. Many buildings have VFDs installed but running at fixed speeds, which captures almost none of the possible savings.

**2. Chiller sequencing based on actual load, not fixed staging.** Running the right number of chillers for current demand — and running each of them at their most efficient loading point — typically yields double-digit percentage savings over fixed-stage operation.

**3. Demand-based AHU control.** Tying AHU fan speed and damper position to actual zone temperature and CO₂ levels, rather than a clock, cuts energy during partial-occupancy hours without any comfort tradeoff.

**4. Continuous monitoring with a fast feedback loop.** The fastest way to catch drift — a sensor reading wrong, a valve stuck open, a schedule that didn't update after a floor plan change — is a system that's watching continuously, not a quarterly facility audit.

## The retrofit path

None of the above requires ripping out existing chillers, AHUs or pumps. The equipment already installed in most buildings is capable of running far more efficiently than it does — the gap is almost always in how it's being controlled, not the hardware itself. At [IIT Jammu](/case-studies/iit-jammu), bringing 7 AHUs and 2 chillers under active AI control — on top of existing equipment — delivered 25% campus-wide HVAC energy savings, verified against 35 continuously monitored energy meters.

If you're evaluating where to start, the highest-leverage first step is usually visibility: metering the plant and major AHUs so you can actually see where the load is going, before deciding what to automate first.
